# Multi-Paper Workflow

## Goal

Turn a set of papers into a coherent learning map. Do not write several equally long reports before understanding how the papers relate.

## Step 1: Inventory

For each paper, identify:
- title, authors, year, venue/version;
- field and genre;
- research question;
- main contribution;
- method/theory/study type;
- evidence type;
- accessible materials and missing sections.

## Step 2: Relationship map

Classify relationships only when supported:
- foundational work;
- direct extension;
- competing approach;
- application of a shared method;
- benchmark/dataset/resource;
- survey or synthesis;
- independent work on a related problem.

Use citations, explicit statements, or clear technical comparison. Do not infer a direct lineage from topic similarity alone.

## Step 3: Learning dependency map

Identify which paper or concept helps explain another. Explain why the proposed order is pedagogically useful.

Possible order:
1. foundational concepts or seminal framework;
2. core method or theory;
3. improvements or competing methods;
4. applications and evaluations;
5. surveys for consolidation.

This is a heuristic, not a mandatory chronological order.

## Step 4: Collection-level value and priority

Score each paper using the value framework. Compare papers on:
- unique contribution;
- prerequisite value;
- overlap/redundancy;
- evidence quality;
- beginner learning value.

Do not assume the newest paper is the most important.

## Step 5: Reading plan

Create a table:

| Order | Paper | Role in collection | Priority | Target mastery | Why this order |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Then recommend:
- which paper gets a full deep report first;
- which papers need only orientation;
- which comparisons should be made after reading;
- which background concepts should be learned once.

## Step 6: Comparative synthesis

When comparing papers, align:
- problem definition;
- assumptions;
- inputs/outputs;
- method or argument;
- objective/measurement;
- evidence;
- limitations;
- claimed and demonstrated contributions.

Use side-by-side tables only where the compared dimensions are genuinely comparable.

## Step 7: Avoid false synthesis

- Do not merge different definitions or metrics.
- Do not compare results from incompatible datasets or conditions as if directly comparable.
- Do not claim one paper improves another unless the relationship is established.
- Identify contradictions and unresolved disagreements.
- Separate the collection's shared themes from each paper's unique contribution.
